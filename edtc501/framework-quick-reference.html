1. Biggs' Constructive Alignment (2014)

The Core Idea

Learning outcomes are specified first, and teaching activities and assessments are deliberately designed to support those outcomes. All three components should require the same kind of thinking.

The Coherence Test (Alignment Check)

Ask yourself these three questions:

  1. Can you draw a direct line from your outcome to your assessment?
    • Does the assessment measure what the outcome names?
    • Do they require the same cognitive action (analyze, apply, evaluate)?
  2. Can you explain why the activities develop the specific capability the outcome names?
    • Do students practice the same kind of thinking they'll be assessed on?
    • Or do activities practice recall while assessment requires analysis?
  3. If a student did the activities perfectly, would they be ready for the assessment?
    • If the answer is "maybe" or "not quite," you have an alignment gap.

The Three Alignment Gaps

(Based on Dr. Okafor Scenario, Module 4 Page 4)

Gap Type What It Looks Like Example
Outcome Gap The outcome is too vague to guide design ("understand," "know," "be familiar with") "Students will understand research methods" → What does understanding look like?
Activity Gap Students practice one thing, get assessed on another Students watch lectures and take quizzes (recall) → Final project requires methodological justification (reasoning)
Assessment Gap Assessment measures something easier or different than the outcome names Outcome: "Apply ethical principles" → Assessment: Multiple-choice recall of ethical definitions

The Backwash Principle

What gets assessed is what gets learned. Students rationally respond to what is assessed, not necessarily to what is taught. If assessment measures recall, students will prioritize recall—even if teaching emphasizes analysis.

Design implication: Align your assessment first. Everything else follows.

2. Wiggins & McTighe's Backward Design (2005)

The Three-Stage Sequence

Design in this order (even if you don't teach in this order):

Stage 1: Identify Desired Results

  • What should students understand and be able to do?
  • What are the big ideas (enduring understandings)?
  • What essential questions will guide inquiry?

Key tool: The Stage 1 Template includes established goals, essential questions, understandings, and knowledge/skills.

Stage 2: Determine Acceptable Evidence

  • What would count as evidence of understanding?
  • What performance tasks would demonstrate the learning?
  • What other evidence do you need (quizzes, observations, reflections)?

Key question: How will you know students achieved the results? Don't wait until the end to figure this out.

Stage 3: Plan Learning Experiences

  • What knowledge and skills do students need?
  • What activities will equip them?
  • How will you sequence for maximum effectiveness?

Why "Backward" Matters

Forward design (common but problematic):
Content → Activities → Assessment → Hope

Backward design:
Desired Results → Evidence → Learning Experiences → Success

The difference: Forward design asks "What will I teach?" Backward design asks "What will they learn and how will I know?"

3. WHERETO Elements (Stage 3 Learning Plan)

Use this checklist when designing your learning sequence:

Element Question to Ask Design Move
W - Where & Why Do students know where the module is going and what's expected? Share outcomes, essential questions, and assessment criteria up front
H - Hook What will hook students' interest from the start? Entry event, provocative question, relevant problem
E - Explore & Equip Do students get to explore big ideas and get equipped with needed knowledge/skills? Balance direct instruction with inquiry; provide resources and tools
R - Rethink & Revise Can students rethink their work and revise based on feedback? Build in checkpoints, peer review, formative assessment with feedback
E - Evaluate Can students self-assess and evaluate their own learning? Rubrics shared early, reflection prompts, self-assessment tools
T - Tailored Is the work personalized to different needs, interests, styles? Choice in topics, formats, pace; scaffolding for diverse learners
O - Organized Is the sequence organized for maximum engagement and effectiveness? Logical flow, clear transitions, intentional pacing

4. Productive vs. Unproductive Difficulty

Productive Difficulty (Keep This)

Struggle that develops learning:

  • Complex case that resists easy resolution
  • Ambiguous scenario requiring judgment
  • Assignment demanding genuine synthesis
  • Cognitive challenge inherent to the discipline

Example: "Students struggle to determine which statistical test to use" → This is productive; they're learning to make methodological judgments

Unproductive Difficulty (Remove This)

Struggle that wastes cognitive effort:

  • Unclear instructions
  • Inconsistent navigation
  • Hidden expectations
  • Activities disconnected from assessment
  • Design confusion masquerading as rigor

Example: "Students struggle because the rubric wasn't shared until after submission" → This is unproductive friction

The Friction Audit Question: Where are students spending effort that does not develop the thinking this course is trying to build? Those are the friction points to eliminate.

5. AI as Design Partner Workflow

Use this four-step process when using AI to draft course design elements:

  1. Ask AI to draft
    • "Generate three measurable learning outcomes for a module on [topic]"
    • "Create a rubric for assessing [skill/understanding]"
  2. Critique the output for alignment, rigor, and context
    • Apply the coherence test: Is the outcome specific enough?
    • Check cognitive demand: Does it match my goals?
    • Test disciplinary fit: Does this work in my context?
  3. Revise using professional judgment
    • Change vague verbs ("understand") to action verbs ("apply," "analyze")
    • Add disciplinary specificity
    • Adjust for your students' level and needs
  4. Explain why the final design is stronger
    • "I changed X to Y because..."
    • Ground your revision in the frameworks
    • Document your reasoning for future reference

What AI Can't Do: Determine what matters in your discipline, understand your students' specific needs, or make pedagogical judgments about what constitutes "good enough" evidence.

6. The Three Design Principles

Carry these forward into your redesign:

Principle 1: Start with what students should be able to do

  • Not "What content will I cover?"
  • But "What will students be able to demonstrate?"
  • Outcomes name performances, not topics

Principle 2: Aligned courses require the same kind of thinking at every stage

  • Outcome names a cognitive action (analyze)
  • Activities practice that action (analyze cases)
  • Assessment measures that action (analyze a new case)
  • Not identical, but coherent

Principle 3: Friction that doesn't develop learning should be eliminated

  • Distinguish productive challenge from design confusion
  • Remove unnecessary complexity in instructions, navigation, expectations
  • Protect difficulty that builds disciplinary thinking

Quick Application Guide

For Your Discussion Post (Identifying Misalignment)

  1. Choose a module from your course
  2. Apply the coherence test: State outcome, describe activities, name assessment
  3. Identify the gap: Outcome gap? Activity gap? Assessment gap?
  4. Name the learner impact: What does this cost students?

For Your Module Blueprint Assignment

Stage 1 (Steps 1-2): Use Backward Design Stage 1

  • Describe original module
  • Revise outcomes using coherence test
  • Show AI draft → your revision → rationale

Stage 2 (Step 3): Create alignment table

  • Use three-gap framework to diagnose
  • Be honest about where alignment breaks

Stage 3 (Steps 4-6): Design learning sequence

  • Use WHERETO as checklist
  • Audit for unproductive difficulty (friction)
  • Defend using one of the three principles

Self-Check Questions

Before submitting your discussion or blueprint, ask yourself:

Alignment Check

  • ☐ Can I draw a direct line from outcome to assessment?
  • ☐ Do activities practice what the assessment measures?
  • ☐ Is the cognitive demand consistent across all three?

Backward Design Check

  • ☐ Did I start with desired results (Stage 1)?
  • ☐ Have I specified what counts as evidence (Stage 2)?
  • ☐ Are activities designed to produce that evidence (Stage 3)?

Framework Application Check

  • ☐ Have I explicitly referenced at least one framework from the readings?
  • ☐ Can I explain WHY my design choices will work (not just WHAT they are)?
  • ☐ Have I used course vocabulary (coherence test, three-gap diagnostic, WHERETO)?

Professional Judgment Check

  • ☐ If I used AI, have I critically revised its output?
  • ☐ Can I justify my revisions using the frameworks?
  • ☐ Have I considered my specific students and context?

Remember: These frameworks are tools for thinking, not recipes to follow rigidly. Use professional judgment to adapt them to your discipline, students, and context. The goal is coherence, not perfection.
